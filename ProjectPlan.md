Overview: Our group’s goal is to compare how weather conditions affect flight delays and cancellations and compare U.S. airlines in terms of their reliability and if higher cost airlines guarantee on time departures. We will be using three datasets from the Bureau of Transportation Statistics (BTS) and the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA). These datasets dive into airline on-time performance, which includes departures and arrival delays, cancellations, and the airlines responsible for each flight; average airline ticket prices on different routes; and historical weather conditions such as wind speed, precipitation, and visibility. Our plan is to explore each dataset individually to understand its structure and identify which attributes overlap amongst each dataset, such as airport locations, dates, and flight routes. We will clean each dataset separately and combine them into one file we can compare flight performance, ticket prices, and weather conditions. We can then generate visualizations to see patterns, such as in certain weather conditions being connected to more delays or cancelations. We anticipate for there to be some challenges when dealing with data integration, specifically due to the weather dataset uses weather stations rather than airport codes, and ticket pricing dataset reports average fares by airline and route rather than individual flights. There is also varying reporting periods, missing values, and different levels of details across the datasets. This can be eased through thorough cleaning and careful consideration to ensure accurate comparisons. Once that is complete, we will better be able to analyze the relationship between weather, airline performance, and ticket prices and put it all together to answer our research question: How do weather conditions affect flight delays and cancellations, and to what extent do higher-priced U.S. airlines offer more reliable flight experiences?

Datasets: In order to compare accessibility and information provided to the community across museums, we will use datasets from the National Gallery of Art (NGA), the Metropolitan Museum of Art (Met), and the Art Institute of Chicago (AIC). The NGA dataset consists of CSV files documented through a SQL-style data dictionary. We will use `objects.csv` for core object records (objectID, title, locationID, provenanceText), `locations.csv` and `preferred_locations.csv` for physical accessibility (publicAccess flags, site, room, public-facing descriptions), and `objects_constituents.csv` joined with `constituents.csv` for artist and donor relationships. `published_images.csv` adds image-level access through its `openaccess` flag and IIIF URLs. These files link via `objectID` and `locationID`.The Met provides only one CSV export containing all available fields. Columns we will use are `Object ID`, `Is Public Domain`, `Is Highlight`, `Department`, `Culture`, `Medium`, `Artist Display Name`, `Credit Line`, and `Link Resource`. This dataset demonstrates digital accessibility through public domain status, and shows cultural representation, but because it is a single flat file, comparisons are limited to columns within it. `Object ID` serves as its linking key. The AIC dataset is organized as folders of JSON files. We can use the `artworks` folder for object records and public domain status, `agents` for creator data, `galleries` for physical space information, `exhibitions` for programming context, `images` for image access and licensing, and `category-terms` for thematic classification. These link via `id`, with `gallery_id` and `image_id` connecting artworks to spaces and images. When used all together, these datasets can allow us to compare the physical accessibility (NGA locations, AIC galleries), digital accessibility (Met public domain, AIC image licensing, NGA open access), and content of cultural information (NGA provenance, Met cultural fields, AIC agent and exhibition data). Since these are separate institutions, we will mostly be comparing rather than concatenating the data. Object IDs link internal tables, while aggregate metrics enable cross-museum analysis of accessibility and community-facing information.


Constraints:
A major constraint within this project is the difference in which the three museums collect and organize data, making comparisons more difficult. Each dataset was created uniquely to the museum for their own purpose, so while one museum may display some information, the other may not. This limits the amount of attributes that are comparable across the three museums. Additionally, the datasets alter in format, the NGA and the Met use CSV files, while the AIC uses JSON files. This leads to a potential of technical challenges when it is time to clean and organize the data into consitent categories. Another limitation when working with these datasets is their main focus on providing digital information about museums collections and spaces. This means the data will have less information capturing accessibility in a physical sense, such as wheelchair access, visitor expereince, etc. Finally, any incomplete or missing values could also affect results, making us account for all differences when analyzing and comparing the museums.

Gaps:
Before making any more progress on this project there are a few gaps that need to be addressed about the datasets we chose and our analysis approach. First off, and most importantly, we need to define what airline reliability means in terms of our research question. Reliability is able to be measured in various ways, including percentage of flights delayed, average length of delays, or frequency of cancellations. We also need to define what we deem to be a more expensive airline and compare ticket prices fairly, since prices vary when routes, time of year, and other factors come into play. This is important because our goal is to investigate if paying at a premium for certain airlines can translate into stronger reliability with the flight experience. Another step we have to focus on is how to connect the weather data to the flight data. Flight data uses airports and departure times, while weather data uses weather station locations and observation times. To connect this, we can correlate which weather stations are closest to airports and match weather conditions to the corresponding flights. Another gap is determining how we will account for other factors that can cause delays, such as flight crews, air traffic, etc. Finally, we need to standardize our comparison methods and address differences in dataset size, reporting periods, and missing values to ensure that our findings are accurate and meaningful. Overall, the next steps for this project is to address these gaps, so each team member is on the same page about what our project is addressing and our research goals. This will also ensure that we move forward with a standardized approach to our data analysis and cleaning.
