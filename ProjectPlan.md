Datasets: In order to explore the relationship between weather conditions, airline reliability, and ticket prices, we will combine three distinct datasets from the Bureau of Transportation Statistics (BTS) and the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA). The combination of these datasets will allow for a comprehensive analysis of flight performance under various weather scenarios and across different airlines. Sourced from the BTS TranStats database, this first dataset is the focal point of our flight performance analysis. It contains detailed, flight-level information, including flight identifiers which report airline, flight number, and tail number.It contains route information such as origin and destination airports. Further, it includes scheduled and actual departure and arrival times. In addition, it includes performance metrics such as departure and arrival delays (in minutes), cancellation codes, and the cause of delays (for example carrier, weather, National Aviation System). Finally, it includes flight date and day of the week. Our second dataset focuses on average domestic airline prices within the U.S., and the important features include the origin and destination airports which describe the route for which the price is reported, the airline, and the average price for a route and airline given a specific quarter. This information addresses the part of our research question discussing whether more expensive airlines are more reliable. Finally, our third dataset on historical hourly weather data includes meteorological observations in the U.S. and includes the location of the weather stations where the information was reported, the date and hour of the observation, and specific weather condition details. These features allow us to see how weather at or near airports have impacted flight delays. The flight data uses specific airport codes, whereas the weather data is connected to weather station locations. We will combat this by mapping each airport to its nearest weather station, using provided coordinates to ensure the most relevant weather data is associated with each flight. To add on, the flight data is recorded at the individual flight level, while the weather data is hourly. We will align these by matching flight departure times to the corresponding hourly weather observation. Furthermore, the ticket price data is reported quarterly, while flight data is daily. We will aggregate flight performance data to a quarterly level to match the pricing data for our analysis of airline reliability. We also plan on limiting our analysis to a common time frame (a specific year or set of years) where all three datasets have complete and overlapping data. We also have to explicitly define our metrics for "reliability" (for example, percentage of flights delayed) and "higher-priced" airlines (average fare per mile) to make sure our comparisons are consistent.

Constraints:
A major constraint within this project is that each dataset includes data from different time periods. It will be important to only use data from a consistent time range from each of the three datasets. It is also important to include only airlines that match with a weather station in the dataset and that have enough records and information to make meaningful conclusions. One more constraint is that different airlines might more frequently operate out of airports at locations with severe weather, so it will be important to compare the airlines fairly and account for these differences.

First off, and most importantly, we need to define what airline reliability means in terms of our research question. Reliability is able to be measured in various ways, including percentage of flights delayed, average length of delays, or frequency of cancellations. We also need to define what we deem to be a more expensive airline and compare ticket prices fairly, since prices vary when routes, time of year, and other factors come into play. Another step we have to focus on is how to connect the weather data to the flight data. Flight data uses airports and departure times, while weather data uses weather station locations and observation times. To connect this, we can correlate which weather stations are closest to airports and match weather conditions to the corresponding flights. Another gap is determining how we will account for other factors that can cause delays, such as flight crews, air traffic, etc. Finally, we need to standardize our comparison methods and address differences in dataset size, reporting periods, and missing values to ensure that our findings are accurate and meaningful.
